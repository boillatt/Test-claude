# The Next Decade of Western Healthcare: From Hospital Walls to Homes, Screens, and Algorithms

## TL;DR

- **Care is decentralizing.** Over the next ten years, Western health systems are structurally shifting acute and chronic care out of hospitals into homes, communities, and virtual settings — a shift driven by aging populations, unsustainable workforce shortages, and cost pressure, and now locked in by durable policy (the US Acute Hospital Care at Home waiver extended to September 30, 2030; England's NHS "hospital to community" and "analogue to digital" shifts).
- **The dominant early technological response is AI plus remote monitoring.** Capital and clinical adoption are concentrating in ambient AI documentation, AI diagnostics (especially radiology), agentic administrative automation (prior authorization/revenue cycle), and remote patient monitoring — the tools that most directly attack the labor shortage and administrative burden created by the macro shifts.
- **Adoption is real but uneven and financially concentrated.** US digital health raised $14.2B in 2025, with AI-enabled companies capturing 54% of dollars, and the FDA has now authorized ~1,450 AI-enabled devices. But funding is concentrated in a handful of megadeals, evidence quality lags marketing, and reimbursement/regulatory uncertainty (US telehealth flexibilities only extended to end-2027) remains the key gating factor.

## Key Findings

1. **Three structural shifts define the decade**: (a) site-of-care migration from hospitals to home/ambulatory/virtual settings; (b) primary care's move toward value-based, longitudinal, team-based "advanced primary care"; and (c) integrated/neighbourhood care models that fuse health and social care. These are responses to the same root causes — demographic aging, chronic disease burden, workforce shortages, and cost.
1. **The workforce shortage is the master driver.** WHO projects a shortfall of 10 million health workers globally by 2030 (Boniol et al., *BMJ Global Health*, June 2022, revising the 2016 estimate of 18 million down to "15 million health workers in 2020 decreasing to 10 million health workers by 2030"; WHO's later reassessment has since raised the near-term figure to ~11 million). WHO's Regional Office for Europe projects a shortfall of 943,000 health workers in the WHO European Region by 2030 — reaching 1.3 million when personal-care workers are included (*Health workforce migration in the WHO European Region*, September 16, 2025). The US faces a projected shortage of between 13,500 and 86,000 physicians by 2036 (AAMC, *The Complexities of Physician Supply and Demand: Projections From 2021 to 2036*, March 2024, including 20,200–40,400 primary care physicians), and a nursing gap that HRSA's 2021–2036 projection put at 337,970 full-time-equivalent RNs (a 9% shortage). England's NHS projects a staff shortfall of 260,000–360,000 FTE by 2036/37 if no action is taken (NHS *Long Term Workforce Plan*, June 30, 2023). Because you cannot train clinicians fast enough, systems are turning to technology and task-shifting to caregivers and patients themselves.
1. **Behaviorally, all four stakeholder groups are being pushed toward more self-service and data-sharing.** Patients increasingly behave like consumers (digital-first access, willingness to pay for personalization); clinicians are being relieved of documentation but face new oversight/validation burdens; family caregivers are absorbing more complex clinical tasks; and payers/administrators are deploying AI for prior authorization and revenue cycle.
1. **Early solutions cluster in five categories**: ambient AI scribes; AI diagnostics/clinical decision support; remote patient monitoring and hospital-at-home enablement; agentic administrative automation (prior auth, coding, revenue cycle); and patient/caregiver engagement platforms.
1. **Market signals point to consolidation around AI infrastructure.** 2025 saw a funding rebound concentrated in fewer, larger, AI-focused deals; a reopening IPO window; and surging M&A. Europe's DiGA "app on prescription" model and NHS digital plan, plus Canadian provincial home-care investment, show parallel but distinct trajectories.

## Details

### 1. Structural changes in care delivery

**From hospital to home.** The clearest structural signal is the migration of acute care into the home. McKinsey estimated that up to $265 billion of care services for Medicare fee-for-service and Medicare Advantage beneficiaries could shift from traditional facilities to the home by 2025 — a three- to fourfold increase in the cost of care delivered at home for that population. The US "Acute Hospital Care at Home" (AHCAH) waiver, launched by CMS in November 2020 as a COVID response, became the policy vehicle. After a series of short-term extensions and a disruptive 43-day lapse during the fall 2025 government shutdown, Section 6210 of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (P.L. 119-75) extended the waiver through September 30, 2030 — giving health systems a genuine five-year planning horizon for the first time.

The evidence base is now strong enough to justify the bet: a September 2024 CMS study found hospital-at-home patients had lower mortality than matched brick-and-mortar inpatients  across the top 25 MS-DRGs (statistically significant for 11 of 25),  with mixed readmission results and comparable hospital-acquired-condition rates. A Mass General Brigham/Harvard national analysis of Medicare claims found low mortality and readmission that persisted across demographics, disability, and dual-eligibility status.  A UK propensity-matched cohort study found an odds ratio for all-cause mortality of 0.43   and for 30-day readmission of 0.55,   with ~3 bed-days saved per episode.

**From hospital to community (Europe).** England's "Fit for the Future: 10 Year Health Plan," published July 3, 2025, is built on three shifts: "hospital to community," "analogue to digital," and "sickness to prevention."  It commits to a Neighbourhood Health Service, an ambition that the majority of outpatient care happen outside hospitals by 2035,  a single patient record, and reserving at least 3% of annual spend for one-time service-transformation investments.  Independent commentary (*BMJ*) is skeptical that the money follows the rhetoric: health spending grows 2.8%/year in real terms (below the 3.7% historic average) and capital just 1%/year.

**Value-based and advanced primary care (US).** CMS introduced Advanced Primary Care Management (APCM) in 2025  via three new billing codes (G0556–G0558), designed to move primary care from fee-for-service toward risk-stratified, longitudinal, capitated-style payment. This continues a decade of models (CPC, CPC+, Primary Care First, Making Care Primary, ACO REACH). Analysts warn independent primary care faces near-extinction by 2030 under fee-for-service economics unless practices shift to value-based models.

**Remote patient monitoring as connective tissue.** RPM is the enabling layer for both home-based acute care and chronic disease management. Market sizing varies widely by source (a sign of an immature category): estimates for the global RPM market in 2024–2025 range from roughly $9B to over $40B, but all project double-digit CAGRs (~18–25%) through the early-to-mid 2030s. North America dominates (~48% share in 2025), driven by CMS reimbursement codes (99453/99454/99457/99458).

### 2. Behavioral impacts

**Patients/consumers.** PwC's 2025 Health Consumer Survey (4,030 US consumers) found seven in ten consumers already use health technology monthly (wearables, apps, portals), and at least one in five would pay more out of pocket for personalized treatment (28%), continuous monitoring (22%), or unified digital platforms (19%) — with willingness highest among Gen Z and Millennials. Consumers increasingly arrive "with a self-diagnosis" and expect bidirectional data-sharing partnership rather than a passive patient role. A major behavioral caveat: digital engagement is shallow — per the NCT04507360 study protocol (Enhancing Engagement With Digital Mental Health Care), "the average length of engagement is only 2–4 weeks, with most consumers discontinuing use within hours of downloading."

**Healthcare providers (HCPs).** The dominant behavioral story is documentation relief and role change. Physician burnout has declined for the fourth straight year — 41.9% reported at least one burnout symptom in 2025, down from 43.2% (2024) and 48.2% (2023) per the AMA — partly attributable to workflow tools. But clinicians face new burdens: validating AI outputs, monitoring for hallucinations, and adapting to AI that is expanding from note-taking into coding and revenue-cycle roles, raising questions about who benefits. Burnout remains higher in hospital-employed physicians and in emergency medicine, urological surgery, and radiology. Replacing a nurse averages ~$56,300 and physician turnover $500,000–$1 million (KLAS), which is itself a powerful economic driver of workflow-technology adoption.

**Caregivers.** Family and professional caregivers are absorbing complex clinical tasks as care moves home. In 2025, 63 million Americans — nearly 1 in 4 adults — were family caregivers, a 45% jump from 2015, of whom 47.8 million are unpaid, per AARP/National Alliance for Caregiving *Caregiving in the US 2025* (July 24, 2025). The economic scale is staggering: the 59 million caregivers of adults provided 49.5 billion hours of care in 2024, valued at $1.01 trillion ($20.41/hour), per AARP *Valuing the Invaluable 2026* (March 26, 2026) — exceeding total 2024 Medicaid spending of $932 billion. Digital tools (caregiver apps, telemonitoring, medication management, LLM-based emotional support) show moderate effectiveness in reducing caregiver burden and stress in the short term, but with high variability. Home-care operators are turning to AI-enabled scheduling, routing, and documentation to stretch a shrinking direct-care workforce (59% of US home-care workers receive some public assistance, per PHI's 2025 Direct Care Workers Key Facts).

**Payers/administrators.** Payers are moving fastest on prior authorization and claims. Per the 2026 HealthEdge Annual Payer Report, 94% of payers have adopted AI, with prior authorization and claims adjudication the highest-impact areas. In June 2025, dozens of insurers pledged to reform prior authorization, committing to issue at least 80% of approvals in real time — a commitment achievable only through automation. CMS's Interoperability and Prior Authorization rule (CMS-0057-F) requires FHIR-based APIs for electronic prior auth by January 2027, and states (Texas, Arizona, Maryland) have passed laws requiring human oversight of AI denials.  The urgency is real: the 2025 AMA Prior Authorization Physician Survey found 95% of physicians said prior auth delays care and practices spend ~13 hours per physician per week on it.

### 3. Early technological and solution responses

**Ambient AI scribes** are "poised to become one of the fastest technology adoptions in healthcare history" (Peterson Health Technology Institute). Evidence: a November 2024–January 2025 randomized trial (238 physicians, 14 specialties, *NEJM AI*) validated ambient scribes, though only Nabla hit statistical significance on time-in-note (−9.5%, P=0.02); DAX Copilot did not differ significantly from control. Real-world deployments: Mass General Brigham observed a 21.2% reduction in burnout after 84 days (*JAMA* 2025); Cleveland Clinic selected Ambience after a five-vendor head-to-head pilot, seeing a 32% increase in patient face-time and a 25% decrease in note-creation time across ~25,000 encounters; Duke deployed Abridge to 5,000 clinicians across 150+ clinics. Critically, the business case has pivoted from clinician well-being to revenue capture through more intensive coding — Abridge's CEO calls it "a mid-revenue cycle company," and Ambience positions itself around "clinical documentation integrity" and "revenue-cycle performance."

**AI diagnostics and clinical decision support.** The FDA has authorized ~1,451 AI-enabled medical devices since 1995 (through end-2025), with radiology accounting for ~76% (1,104 devices); GE HealthCare leads with 120 radiology authorizations, followed by Siemens Healthineers (89) and Philips (50). 2025 was a record year, with roughly 295–331 AI/ML device authorizations depending on the counting methodology. Aidoc's CARE1 foundation model received the first foundation-model clearance in February 2025, and RecovryAI won the first Breakthrough Device Designation for a generative-AI (LLM) medical device in late 2025.

**Agentic administrative automation.** Menlo Ventures found providers supply ~75% ($1B of $1.4B) of healthcare AI spend, with health systems leading because ROI is obvious given thin margins and staffing shortages. Near-term use cases are administrative (revenue cycle, prior auth, scheduling), with clinical breakthroughs still speculative.  Vendors such as Cohere Health report up to 8–9x ROI on agentic prior-auth platforms.

**Remote monitoring and hospital-at-home enablers.** Partnerships proliferated in 2025 (e.g., BioIntelliSense + Hicuity Health; Tenovi + Circadian Health and GenieMD), integrating medical-grade wearables with virtual clinical oversight, and layering AI analytics to reduce alert fatigue and data overload.

### 4. Market signals and adoption patterns

**Venture funding rebounded and concentrated in AI.** US digital health startups raised $14.2B in 2025 (Rock Health), up 35% over 2024's $10.5B and the highest since 2022. AI-enabled companies captured 54% of dollars (up from 37%), commanding a ~19% deal-size premium; 50% of all deals were closed by AI-enabled companies. But the market is a "tale of two markets": removing the top nine companies by dollars raised would pull 2025 below 2024. Average deal size rose to $29.3M; there were 26 megadeals and 15 new unicorns (vs. six in 2024). Q1 2026 continued the trend, with $4B raised but ~60% from just 12 megadeals. Broader trackers concur: CB Insights put global digital health funding at $22.3B (all 14 of its new unicorns AI-native); Galen Growth at $28.8B (+9%), with Europe fastest-growing (+15%).

**IPO window reopened; M&A surged.** Five digital health companies IPO'd in 2025 (Hinge Health, Omada Health, Heartflow, Carlsmed, Profusa) after a three-year drought; M&A rose ~61% to 195 deals (Rock Health; CB Insights logged 210, +33%). Landmark AI scribe raises: Abridge's $300M Series E at a $5.3B valuation (months after a $250M Series D); Ambience Healthcare's $243M Series C co-led by Oak HC/FT and Andreessen Horowitz. Consumer preventive-health also drew conviction capital: Finland's Oura raised a $900M round at an $11B valuation, Function Health $298M, and Sweden's Neko Health $260M.

**Regulatory signals are the key swing factor.** US Medicare telehealth flexibilities were extended only through December 31, 2027 (Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026), after lapsing twice in late 2025  — telehealth use remains roughly twice pre-pandemic levels, with more than 1 in 10 traditional Medicare beneficiaries using it in the first half of 2025. Behavioral/mental telehealth flexibilities are permanent; most others are not. This short runway is the single biggest source of uncertainty for virtual-care business models. The CONNECT for Health Act of 2025 would make them permanent but has not advanced.

### Geographic variation

- **US:** Market-driven, reimbursement-gated, fastest AI adoption; hospital-at-home and value-based care advancing but politically contingent.
- **UK:** State-directed transformation via the NHS 10-Year Plan; strong central ambition ("digital by default," virtual wards, five transformative technologies: data, AI, genomics, wearables, robotics) but constrained capital and workforce (260,000–360,000 projected FTE shortfall by 2036/37).
- **Germany/EU:** The DiGA "app on prescription" model is the world's most developed statutory reimbursement pathway for digital therapeutics — as of end-2025, 58 reimbursable DiGAs, with 2025 alone seeing nearly 700,000 prescriptions (~€171M in spending) and cumulative prescriptions surpassing 1 million by end-2024. From 2026, at least 20% of the reimbursement price will depend on measured real-world outcomes, and eligibility extends to higher-risk Class IIb devices. OECD's *Health at a Glance: Europe 2024* estimated an EU shortage of ~1.2 million doctors, nurses, and midwives as of 2022.
- **Canada:** Provincial home-care investment (Ontario's $1.1B over three years for home care and the Hospital-to-Home program, on top of a 2024 $2B commitment) plus a patchwork of virtual-care regulation varying by province; a Deloitte Canada report (November 2025) found a potential 46% ROI on additional Ontario home-care investment, primarily via reduced alternate-level-of-care days and delayed long-term-care admissions.

## Recommendations

**For health systems / providers (act now):**

1. Treat the AHCAH waiver's 2030 horizon and NHS neighbourhood shifts as a genuine build signal — stand up or scale hospital-at-home and RPM programs, but require 24/7 RN coverage, EMS coordination, and rigorous patient selection.
1. Deploy ambient AI scribes as the highest-ROI, lowest-clinical-risk entry point, but build QA dashboards tracking hallucination rate, omitted negatives, and clinician-edit deltas, and get ahead of the coding/compliance implications before payers recalibrate rates.
1. Prioritize agentic automation of prior authorization and revenue cycle where CMS-0057-F (FHIR APIs by January 2027) creates a structural deadline.

**For payers/administrators:** Invest in real-time prior-auth automation to meet the June 2025 industry pledge, but preserve documented human clinical review to comply with emerging state laws (Texas, Arizona, Maryland).

**For investors/innovators:** Capital is rewarding AI-embedded, workflow-native, outcomes-proven companies; point solutions without EHR integration or evidence face a hostile market. Europe (DiGA's outcomes-linked pricing from 2026) rewards real-world evidence generation.

**Benchmarks that would change the recommendation:**

- If US telehealth flexibilities are made permanent (CONNECT for Health Act)  → accelerate virtual-first investment; if they lapse at end-2027 → hedge.
- If AI-scribe coding practices trigger widespread payer downcoding/audits (Cigna began automatic E/M downcoding in October 2025) → the revenue-capture business case weakens.
- If hospital-at-home reimbursement rates are cut or the 2030 waiver is not renewed → pause capital-intensive home-acute builds.

## Caveats

- **Hype vs. evidence gap.** Marketing (especially in AI and RPM) consistently outpaces peer-reviewed evidence. Many RPM market-size figures come from commercial research firms with wide, inconsistent ranges and should be treated as directional, not precise.
- **Financial concentration.** The 2025 funding "rebound" is concentrated in a handful of megadeals; the broad startup ecosystem remains stressed, with 600+ companies in funding limbo since 2021–22.
- **Engagement is shallow.** Consumer enthusiasm in surveys does not translate to sustained engagement; digital tools show high drop-off.
- **Policy risk is acute.** US telehealth and hospital-at-home authorities are temporary and have already lapsed during shutdowns; the whole virtual/home-care edifice rests on reimbursement decisions renewed in short increments.
- **Equity risk.** Decentralized, digital-first care risks widening disparities for patients lacking broadband, devices, digital literacy, or family caregivers.
- **Forward-looking projections** (workforce shortfalls, market CAGRs, spending shifts) are model-based estimates, not certainties, and are sensitive to assumptions — e.g., AAMC's physician-shortage range narrows or widens entirely based on assumed growth in graduate medical education funding, and HRSA's nursing projections differ materially between its 2021–2036 and 2023–2038 report vintages.
